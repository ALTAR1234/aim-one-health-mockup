This workflow turns one genetic variant into a ranked list of gene-therapy options, each with its reason for failing, before any lab money is spent. The post is open about where the model is weakest.

What a "virtual cell" is here

It's a computer model of only the biology needed for the question. Here that's a simple chain: gene to mRNA, mRNA to protein, protein to neuron function. The patient's mutation breaks the first link.

A few linked equations track therapy mRNA, the protein made from it, and a neuron-function score that depends on protein level and treatment timing. On top sits a rescue score:

molecular rescue = delivery × on-target action × full-length yield × NMD-escape × activity

Each factor is a probability drawn from a range, so each simulated experiment is 20,000 draws giving a spread of outcomes. NMD (nonsense-mediated decay) is the cell's system for destroying faulty mRNA; NMD-escape is the share that survives.

The case the virtual cell was built to answer

Synthetic case · SYN-NEXMIF-0001

A boy has a nonsense variant in NEXMIF (c.602C>A, p.Ser201Ter), a brain gene neurons need to grow and connect. The early stop means the mRNA is likely destroyed, leaving about 0% working protein. The gene is on the X chromosome, so he has no backup copy.

The model's inputs were fetched from primary sources, not recalled:

  • Transcript NM_001008537.3 (NCBI).
  • Stop codon 3,856 nucleotides before the last exon junction, so NMD is expected.
  • ~3.5× higher expression in brain (GTEx).
  • Almost no tolerance for loss of function (gnomAD pLI ≈ 1.0, LOEUF 0.23).

Running the rescue analysis

Each therapy was plugged into the same model with its own five factors. Gene reintroduction gives a clean copy (high yield, high NMD-escape) but is limited by delivery. Readthrough has very low yield because it can't rebuild the missing protein. Every scenario ran 20,000 draws over 180 simulated days, across delivery levels (poor to brain-wide) and ages (infant to adult).

  • Gene reintroduction: 41% at base delivery, 79% brain-wide.
  • Prime editing: 14% → 30%.
  • Readthrough and HDR: under ~3.4%, not viable, each failing on a named factor.

The iteration loop — and the moment it earned trust

The time-based model and the simple five-factor product must agree at steady state. If they don't, the model is adding behavior nobody can audit.

The first version didn't agree. A 1.05 expression multiplier gave 43% and 83% instead of the reference 41% and 79%, quietly inventing 2–4 points of rescue. Setting it to 1.0 made the results match exactly. Now the simulator adds only timing on top of the transparent model.

Every result breaks down into five named factors, and its end point can be checked by hand.

Making confidence concrete

The inputs are priors, not this patient's measurements. So instead of defending 79%, the team found what the answer depends on most and named the test that settles it.

Ask what dominates the uncertainty

A Sobol analysis over 200,000 simulations showed CNS delivery explains ~91% of the variance for gene reintroduction. Everything else adds up to ~9%.

Quantify what a measurement buys

Of the ±11.9-point total spread, measuring CNS delivery removes ±11.4. No other single test comes close.

Corroborate with an independent model

Evo2, a 7-billion-parameter genomic model that knows nothing about the patient, also rated the change disruptive (−1.3479 mutant vs −1.3401 normal). It ranked the variant 7th of 27 possible changes at that spot (~78th percentile).

Confidence here doesn't mean certainty. It means the model knows where it is weak and what would fix it.

The interventions the model ranks, and why

Gene reintroduction — leadAdds a working copy. 41% → 79%. Hinges on delivery (91% of variance); has in-vivo precedent.
Prime editing — backupFixes the exact typo. 14% → 30%. Hinges on editing efficiency (~60%), then delivery (~35%).
Readthrough & HDR — ruled outUnder ~3.4%. Readthrough fails on full-length yield; HDR fails because non-dividing neurons rarely use that repair route.

Hypotheses tested, and alternatives ruled out

Seven hypotheses framed the work, and negative results counted as much as positive ones.

  • Confirmed: NMD clears the mutant mRNA (H1); an NMD-proof copy is the lead (H2); developmental timing limits benefit even though rescue is flat with age (H5); delivery is the master variable at ~91% (H6); the 25% benefit bar is a sharp cutoff, crossed at ~30% of neurons corrected (H7).
  • Supported: prime editing as a precise backup, 14% → 30% (H3).
  • Ruled out: readthrough and HDR can't reach the bar in non-dividing neurons (H4).

Six competing assumptions were also disproved: base editing can't make the needed A→C change; readthrough and HDR stay under 3.4%; on-target efficiency isn't the lead's key lever; the 1.05 multiplier inflated the result; and protein alone doesn't guarantee benefit at every age.

Where the virtual cell becomes a program, not a paper

Two more layers are needed for a treated child. First, a six-discipline review (medical genetics, gene-therapy engineering, pediatric neurology, delivery/vectorology, biostatistics, family navigation). Each adds conditions, such as unproven delivery at scale, monitoring for too much protein, and measuring off-targets. These become the risk register.

Second, the Navigator turns the consensus into a family roadmap: a headline, next steps, checkpoints, questions and logistics, drafted as an At-a-Glance card and a Plain-Language Guide.

An honest note on the platform. There's no live "Multidisciplinary" or "Navigator" connector yet. Those layers are agent-simulated, built to the format a real system would accept, so going live means connecting an endpoint, not redoing the science.

Why this matters

In rare-disease gene therapy, the prep work before experts weigh in eats the field's scarcest time. This cycle helps by:

  • Replacing debate with simulation: every strategy run on the same cell, ranked with failure reasons.
  • Naming the key lever per approach: delivery for gene reintroduction, editing efficiency for prime editing.
  • Being checkable: it reduces to a hand-checkable model, traces inputs to live databases, is backed by an independent model, and updates when real data arrive.
  • Reaching the family, not just the lab.

AI doesn't decide the therapy. It does the hard routine work reliably, so experts can focus where they matter.

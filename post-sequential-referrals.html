From Sequential Referrals to Multidisciplinary Deliberation

Bringing specialists together earlier can cut delays, improve coordination and help patients make informed treatment choices sooner.

Why Complex Patients Often Need More Than a Referral Chain

Specialization has brought big advances, but it also means complex patients pass through many separate consults before a clear plan appears: referral, test, specialist, another referral, repeat.

Each step makes sense on its own. The trouble is they happen in isolation. Everyone waits, information trickles in, and decisions stall until enough views are collected.

The problem is not a lack of specialists. It's bringing the right expertise together early enough.

The Reality Patients Experience

Say routine imaging shows a suspicious lung lesion:

  • The radiologist spots it.
  • A pulmonologist orders more tests.
  • A pathologist reviews the biopsy later.
  • A surgeon checks whether it can be removed.
  • An oncologist wants molecular results before discussing treatment.

All of this can take weeks or months. Meanwhile the patient asks:

  • Do my doctors all see the same information?
  • Should I already have had other tests?
  • Why wait for another visit before the next decision?
  • What are my real treatment options?
  • Am I losing time?

The same happens in heart, neurological, autoimmune and rare diseases, not just cancer.

The Cost of Sequential Decision-Making

When specialties review a case one at a time, common problems include:

  • Delayed diagnostic answers: needed tests only show up in later visits.
  • Repeated information gathering: patients retell their story at every visit.
  • Fragmented clinical perspectives: imaging, pathology, labs and genetics aren't looked at together.
  • Increased patient anxiety: the gaps between visits are hard on patients and families.
  • Longer time to action: treatment waits until every input is in.

Not every case goes this way, but it's common enough that many health systems are looking for ways to bring experts together sooner.

The Shift Toward Multidisciplinary Deliberation

Instead of consults in a row, radiology, pathology, surgery, other specialties, genetics and care planning weigh in on the same question at the same time. Specialists aren't replaced; the aim is a fuller picture before big decisions. Together, teams can find:

  • Other diagnoses to consider.
  • Gaps in the available information.
  • Alternative treatment paths.
  • Risks that need attention.
  • Ways to coordinate care better.

Above all, key questions come up earlier.

Better Questions Lead to Better Decisions

Often the most useful input isn't one recommendation but asking the right question at the right time:

  • Is more molecular testing needed?
  • Would a second pathology review help?
  • Should there be more imaging before planning?
  • Are there competing approaches to weigh?
  • How do the patient's goals shape the options?

Answering these early lets teams decide with a broader view of the patient and the evidence.

Putting Patients at the Center

Patients don't see their care as a set of specialties. They see one journey and expect their doctors to talk to each other. Deliberating together supports that, helping patients move from uncertainty to action.

The future isn't just more specialists. It's getting the right expertise, information and evidence together sooner, so patients can focus on their health and treatment.
